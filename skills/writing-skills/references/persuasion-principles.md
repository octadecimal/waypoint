# Persuasion principles for discipline skills

This is an attributed summary of external research included with the upstream
Superpowers materials. It is not current Codex product documentation, and its
claims have not been independently revalidated for every Codex model.

The upstream guide cites Robert Cialdini's *Influence* (2021) and Meincke et al.,
"Call Me A Jerk: Persuading AI to Comply with Objectionable Requests" (2025). It
uses their concepts to explain why instruction wording can alter compliance.

Use this only after a baseline shows an agent skips a real rule under pressure.

| Principle | Legitimate skill use |
| --- | --- |
| Authority | Direct language for safety, permission, verification, or discipline boundaries. |
| Commitment | Explicit checkpoints, recorded evidence, and required artifact fields. |
| Scarcity | Sequence requirements such as "before publishing," without false urgency. |
| Social proof | Clear team norms backed by actual policy. |
| Unity | Collaborative language tied to shared quality goals. |

Avoid liking and reciprocity as compliance devices; they encourage sycophancy or
obligation rather than sound judgment. Never invent urgency, consensus, policy, or
authority.

Match wording to the failure:

- A knowingly skipped rule may need a bright line, stop condition, rationalization
  table, and consequence.
- A malformed output needs a positive recipe or schema.
- An omission needs a required slot.
- Context-dependent behavior needs an explicit condition.

The ethical test is simple: would the instruction still serve the user's interest
if they understood exactly how and why it was written?
